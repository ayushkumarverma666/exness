## 1. Emotion and sharing (Berger & Milkman; Brady; Rathje; Robertson)

### Takeaway
High-arousal emotion (awe, anger, anxiety) predicts sharing. Moral-emotional and out-group-hostile language raises retweets and shares. Negative words raise clicks on news headlines. But the per-word effects are modest: moral contagion is about +12–15% per word in the meta-analysis, not the original +20%. Most of the evidence comes from news and political content, not professional networks.

### Cited Findings
- **Berger, J. & Milkman, K. L. (2012). "What Makes Online Content Viral?" *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), DOI 10.1509/jmr.10.0353.** Data: all New York Times articles over about 3 months, using the "most e-mailed" list as the outcome, plus lab experiments. Content that evokes high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger, anxiety) emotion is more viral. Low-arousal, deactivating emotion (sadness) is less viral. The experiments show causality through physiological activation. **[verified via search]** — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Northeastern copy](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf)
- Berger & Milkman effect sizes, as change in the odds of making the most-e-mailed list per 1 SD increase: awe about +30%, anger about +34%, anxiety about +21%, sadness about −16%, practical utility about +30%, interest about +25%, surprise about +14%, positivity about +13%. These are controlled for position on the homepage, author fame and similar factors. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]** — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- The paper won Sage's 10-Year Impact Award and has more than 1,300 citations. — [Social Science Space](https://www.socialsciencespace.com/2023/05/a-viral-paper-on-determining-what-makes-online-content-viral/)
- **Brady, W. J., Wills, J. A., Jost, J. T., Tucker, J. A., & Van Bavel, J. J. (2017). "Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks." *PNAS* 114(28).** Data: about 563k tweets on gun control, same-sex marriage and climate change. Each moral-emotional word was associated with about a 20% increase in retweets (IRR ≈ 1.20). The effect was mostly within ideological in-groups. **[verified via search]** — [Birkbeck critique PDF](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/46928/5/46928.pdf); [CSMaP NYU summary](https://csmapnyu.org/research/academic-research/emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks)
- **Updated effect size: Brady et al. (2025), *PNAS Nexus*.** The authors ran a pre-registered direct replication (N = 849,266) using the dictionary method plus LLM classification, and a meta-analysis of 27 studies from 5 labs. Pooled IRR = 1.12 (95% CI 1.06–1.19); replication IRR = 1.15; original IRR = 1.20. The original study overestimated the effect, but it is real and positive. This is the original team re-estimating their own result. **[verified via search]** — [Kellogg research summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2025/estimating-the-effect-size-of-moral-contagion-in-online)
- A critique applied the 2017 dictionary approach to 6 other tweet corpora and questioned how robust the method is. Burton et al. (2021, *Nature Human Behaviour*) published a reanalysis challenging "moral contagion," but I could not retrieve it. — [Birkbeck eprint](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/46928/5/46928.pdf)
- **Rathje, S., Van Bavel, J. J., & van der Linden, S. (2021). "Out-group animosity drives engagement on social media." *PNAS* 118(26).** Data: about 2.7M Facebook and Twitter posts from news media and US Congress accounts. Each word referring to the political out-group raised the odds of a share or retweet by about 67%. Out-group posts were shared about twice as often as in-group posts. The out-group effect was much stronger than emotional-language effects. **[verified via search]** — [PMC8256037](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8256037/)
- Counterpoint: in an experiment by Kasper & Gilovich, partisans did not prefer derogatory content when asked to share content meant to go viral. This suggests the engagement pattern may not reflect what users intend. — [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/286674184/Do_People_Prefer_to_Share_Political_Information_That_Boosts_Their_Ingroup_or_Derogates_the_Outgroup.pdf)
- **Robertson, C. E., Pröllochs, N., Schwarzenegger, K., Pärnamets, P., Van Bavel, J. J., & Feuerriegel, S. (2023). "Negativity drives online news consumption." *Nature Human Behaviour* 7, 812–822, DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4.** Data: Upworthy headline RCTs from 2013–2015, about 105,000 headline variations, about 5.7M clicks and more than 370M impressions, across 22,743 RCTs. For an average-length headline, each additional negative word increased click-through rate by **2.3%**. Positive words decreased CTR. Sadness words had the strongest effect, ahead of anger and fear. **[verified via search]** — [PMC10202797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10202797/); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
- One secondary source puts the effect of a positive word at about −1.0% CTR. I could not confirm this from the paper. The same source reports no difference across content categories. — [DigitalContentNext](https://digitalcontentnext.org/blog/2023/04/04/do-negative-headlines-drive-news-traffic/)
- The Robertson findings and Berger & Milkman disagree on sadness. Robertson finds sadness words increase clicks. Berger & Milkman find sad content is shared less. Clicking and sharing are different behaviors. — [PMC10202797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10202797/); [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)

### Inferences
- The per-unit effects are single-digit to tens of percent on the odds of engagement, a far smaller lever than follower count or posting time. A creator should treat emotional framing as an increase in the odds, not a guarantee of virality.
- The anger, out-group and negativity findings come mostly from political and news content on Twitter and Facebook. On LinkedIn, hostility can cost a creator reputation. The emotions that fit LinkedIn's norms are awe and inspiration (high-arousal positive), anxiety (for example about career or AI risk) and practical utility, all of which Berger & Milkman found raise sharing.
- The Robertson sadness and negativity effect is about clicks on headlines, which is closest to a LinkedIn hook that earns a "see more" click.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the exact Berger & Milkman coefficient tables or the Burton et al. 2021 reanalysis text.
- I found no study that tests whether emotion effects transfer to LinkedIn.

## 2. Headlines and hooks (Upworthy archive, clickbait, curiosity gap, numbers)

### Takeaway
The Upworthy Research Archive of randomized headline A/B tests from 2013–2015 is the gold-standard dataset. Negative words reliably raise clicks, but most A/B tests have no statistically distinguishable winner. Headline effects are usually small and noisy.

### Cited Findings
- Robertson et al. (2023) used the archive's RCTs, 22,743 of them. Each negative word gave about +2.3% CTR. — [PMC10202797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10202797/)
- The LOLA paper (Ye et al., arXiv 2406.02611) uses 17,618 Upworthy headline tests. — [arXiv 2406.02611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.02611); [UW PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/hemay/LOLA.pdf)
- Most Upworthy A/B tests lack a statistically reliable winner. One 2026 study kept only 399 tests with a clear winner for evaluation. — [arXiv 2609.25010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25010)
- Building pairs where the CTR difference is significant at p < .05 gives 41,624 headline pairs. — [arXiv 2506.00152](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00152v1)
- Upworthy Research Archive dataset paper: Matias, J. N., Munger, K., Le Quere, M. A., & Ebersole, C. (2021), "The Upworthy Research Archive, a time series of 32,487 experiments in U.S. media," *Scientific Data*. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]**

### Inferences
- Since most headline variants do not differ measurably, a creator should test large framing changes such as emotional valence, a specific number, or a named stake, not small wording tweaks.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary studies on the curiosity gap or on numbers and specificity in headlines. Candidates to check are Loewenstein (1994) on curiosity and Kuiken et al. (2017) on Dutch news headlines in *Digital Journalism*. I also found no clickbait-specific effect sizes this session.

## 3. Predictability of cascades (Cheng 2014; Martin 2016; Weng; Goel 2016)

### Takeaway
Predicting the final size of a cascade from the content alone, before it is posted, is fundamentally limited. The best models explain well under half the variance. Prediction gets much better once early dynamics are observed (speed and breadth of the first reshares). Luck and cumulative advantage are large factors.

### Cited Findings
- **Cheng, J., Adamic, L., Dow, P. A., Kleinberg, J., & Leskovec, J. (2014). "Can cascades be predicted?" WWW '14, pp. 925–936, arXiv 1403.4608.** Data: Facebook photo reshare cascades. The paper frames prediction as whether a cascade that has reached k reshares will double to 2k. Prediction is strong for continued growth. Relative growth becomes more predictable as more reshares are observed. Temporal features (speed) and structural features are the key predictors. Early breadth, not depth, signals larger cascades. **[verified via search]** — [arXiv 1403.4608](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1403.4608); [Webis anthology](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2014.wwwconf_conference-2014.83/)
- Cheng et al. report about 0.795 accuracy (against a 0.5 baseline) on the doubling task when observing the first 5 reshares. Temporal features alone come close to all features. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]** — [arXiv 1403.4608](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1403.4608)
- **Martin, T., Hofman, J. M., Sharma, A., Anderson, A., & Watts, D. J. (2016). "Exploring Limits to Prediction in Complex Social Systems." WWW 2016, pp. 683–694, arXiv 1602.01013.** On Twitter cascade size, the best models explain well under half of the variance. Simulations suggest that realistic upper bounds are close to observed performance, because small uncertainty about quality makes the bounds far more restrictive. When predicting before posting, past success of the user dominates content features. **[verified via search]** — [arXiv 1602.01013](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1602.01013); [WWW proceedings PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2016/WWW2016-Proceedings/proceedings/p683.pdf)
- **Goel, S., Anderson, A., Hofman, J., & Watts, D. J. (2016). "The Structural Virality of Online Diffusion." *Management Science* 62(1).** Data: about 1 billion Twitter diffusion events. Large cascades come in a wide range of structures, from a single broadcast hub to true multi-generation viral spread. Size and structural virality are only weakly correlated. Most popular content spreads by broadcast from high-follower accounts, not by person-to-person chains. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]**
- Weng, L., Menczer, F., & Ahn, Y.-Y. (2013). "Virality prediction and community structure in social networks," *Scientific Reports* 3:2522. Memes that spread early across many network communities, rather than staying within one, are more likely to go viral. Early community spread is a strong predictor. Also relevant: Weng et al. (2012), "Competition among memes in a world with limited attention," *Sci. Rep.* 2:335. With limited attention, popularity can be heavy-tailed even without differences in quality. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]**
- Salganik, Dodds & Watts (2006), *Science*, "MusicLab". Social influence increases both the inequality and the unpredictability of success. Quality only weakly bounds outcomes. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]**

### Inferences
- For a creator, the controllable factors are author-level priors (audience size, past performance) and the early distribution of a post: getting fast, diverse early engagement from different communities, such as cross-network comments in the first hours. Content features alone carry limited signal.
- Expect high variance from post to post. Judge a content strategy on distributions over many posts, not on single hits.

### Gaps
- I did not get exact R² values for Martin et al. or exact correlation values for Goel et al. this session.

## 4. Humor, storytelling, social currency, self-disclosure and authenticity

### Takeaway
I could verify little primary evidence this session. Peer-reviewed LinkedIn studies suggest interpersonal content (celebrating others, observances) beats business and self-promotional content on comments and reactions, while business and expertise content gets more reposts.

### Cited Findings
- **Usera, D., Cox, S., & Walker, L. (2026). "What Users Post and Engage With on LinkedIn: A Mixed Methods Study." *SAGE Open*.** Data: posts from 1,001 users coded into 5 themes. Business posts were the most frequent, but Interpersonal and Observance posts drew more comments and reactions. Business and Expertise posts were reposted more often. Highlighting others' achievements drew the most engagement. **[verified via search]** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261428891); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-05-highlighting-linkedin-engagement.html); earlier preprint: [SSRN 4959114](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4959114)
- Social currency, practical utility and storytelling are the STEPPS framework in Berger's *Contagious* (2013). This is a popular book, not peer-reviewed. Practical utility does have support in Berger & Milkman (2012). — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
- Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang (2019), "What Drives Virality (Sharing) of Online Digital Content? The Critical Role of Information, Emotion, and Brand Prominence," *Journal of Marketing* 83(4). As I recall, informative content drives sharing on LinkedIn and Google+, while emotional content drives sharing on YouTube, Facebook and Twitter. **[from paper, not re-verified this session; worth checking because it is directly LinkedIn-relevant]**
- Tamir & Mitchell (2012), *PNAS*, "Disclosing information about the self is intrinsically rewarding." This explains why people post personal content, not why audiences engage with it. **[from paper, not re-verified]**

### Gaps
- I did not search for or verify studies with effect sizes for humor, narrative versus informational content, or authenticity on engagement. This remains a gap.

## 5. LinkedIn-specific academic studies

### Takeaway
There is a small but growing peer-reviewed literature, mostly on samples of about 1,000 posts using count regressions. LinkedIn's own engineering papers cover feed ranking, not content strategy. I found no academic study on "broetry."

### Cited Findings
- **Usera, D. & Durham, N. (2025). "What Predicts Engagement on LinkedIn? Engagement-Boosting Strategies for Professionals." *Business and Professional Communication Quarterly*.** Data: 991 posts analyzed with negative binomial regression. Tagging people and using hashtags significantly increased expected reactions, and tags substantially increased comments. Follower count slightly increased engagement. Post age reduced engagement. Engagement metrics reinforce each other: comments boost reactions and reposts, and so on. **[verified via search]** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23294906251336710)
- B2B company-page studies: "Managing the content of LinkedIn posts: Influence on B2B customer engagement and sales?" (*Journal of Business Research*, 2023), and "B2B social media content: engagement on LinkedIn." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296322008530); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345963882_B2B_social_media_content_engagement_on_LinkedIn)
- Industry, not peer-reviewed: Company Page posts featuring a named person got about 37% more engagement. Comments make up 3.4% of Company Page engagement, against about 20% on personal profiles. The common claim that a comment is worth 15x a like has no traceable primary source. — [leaders.social](https://www.leaders.social/news/linkedin-company-pages-people-news-thought-leadership-engagement-study)
- LinkedIn feed-ranking papers by LinkedIn researchers include Agarwal et al. (KDD 2014), "Activity ranking in LinkedIn feed," and LiRank (arXiv 2402.06859, 2024), on large-scale ranking models. They describe ranking on predicted engagement such as clicks, comments, shares and dwell time. **[from paper, not re-verified this session]**

### Inferences
- Tagging people and featuring others' achievements is the most consistently supported LinkedIn-specific tactic. It overlaps with "social currency" and with the network-breadth result from the cascade literature, since tagged people bring in their own communities.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed work on broetry, on post format (carousel or video versus text), or on dwell time on LinkedIn.

## 6. LLM-based engagement and popularity prediction (2023–2026)

### Takeaway
LLMs have modest but real predictive signal for engagement. They generally do not beat fine-tuned smaller models or encoder classifiers on the same data. Pure prompting on Upworthy headline tests is barely above chance unless the evaluation is restricted to tests with a clear winner.

### Cited Findings
- **Ye et al. (2024). "LOLA: LLM-Assisted Online Learning Algorithm for Content Experiments," arXiv 2406.02611.** On 17,618 Upworthy tests, the authors evaluate pure-LLM headline selection, including a fine-tuned Llama-3 CTR predictor. Their conclusion is that LLMs cannot replace live experimentation but can speed up A/B testing. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.02611); [UW PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/hemay/LOLA.pdf)
- **"Do Synthetic Personas Predict Real Audience Response? A Sim-to-Real Study Where a No-Persona Baseline Beats Persona-Based Copy Simulation" (arXiv 2609.25010, Sept 2026, preprint).** A concurrent study on the same archive found pure-LLM headline prediction only slightly better than random. On the 399 tests with a reliable winner, a no-persona LLM ranker was a medium-strength predictor. Adding synthetic personas removed that advantage. — [arXiv 2609.25010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25010)
- Related Upworthy LLM preprints: a gpt-4o-mini CTR surrogate with a memorization check (arXiv 2606.17165); LoRA-tuned Qwen-0.6B against embedding regression for CTR (arXiv 2608.11912); a causal-alignment perspective (arXiv 2506.00152). — [2606.17165](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17165); [2608.11912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.11912); [2506.00152](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00152v1)
- **"LLM Agents Predict Social Media Reactions but Do Not Outperform Text Classifiers: Benchmarking Simulation Accuracy Using 120K+ Personas of 1511 Humans" (arXiv 2604.19787; published as "Persona-prompted LLM agents achieve modest but genuine prediction of human social media reactions," *Scientific Reports*, 2026).** Predicting like, dislike, comment, share or no reaction across 120K+ persona-post pairs gave MCC = 0.29. This does not beat conventional supervised text classifiers. — [arXiv 2604.19787](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19787); [Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-66277-8)
- **Qiu, Lyu, Xiong & Luo (2025). "Can LLMs Simulate Social Media Engagement? A Study on Action-Guided Response Generation," arXiv 2502.12073.** The models tested were GPT-4o-mini, o1-mini and DeepSeek-R1, predicting retweet, quote or rewrite on X. Zero-shot LLMs did worse than BERT at action prediction, and few-shot prompting at first lowered accuracy. Few-shot LLMs produced responses that were semantically closer to real ones. — [arXiv 2502.12073](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12073v1)
- YouTube comment engagement (Brazil, arXiv 2511.06091): GPT-4.1 reached 81% accuracy with context, while the BERTimbau encoder reached 88% without context. — [arXiv 2511.06091](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.06091)
- Pre-LLM baselines: TwHIN-BERT (arXiv 2209.07562), ViralBERT (arXiv 2206.10298), and RecSys 2020/2021 Twitter engagement challenge work. In that work, user history and the popularity of hashtags and links were the most informative features. — [TwHIN-BERT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07562); [ViralBERT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.10298); [arXiv 2310.03147](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03147)

### Inferences
- Using an LLM to rank a creator's draft hooks is defensible as a weak prior, roughly a coin flip with a modest edge. It should not replace real-world testing, such as posting variants over time and comparing them against the creator's own baseline.
- A plain LLM ranker may beat a persona-based simulated audience. This argues against heavy persona simulation for picking copy.

### Gaps
- Most 2026 items are recent arXiv preprints, and I could not read their full results tables (WebFetch failed). I found no LLM engagement-prediction study on LinkedIn data.

## 7. LLM agent social simulations (OASIS, Y Social, generative agents) and relevance to MiroFish

### Takeaway
OASIS reproduces qualitative phenomena (information spread, polarization, herd effects) but shows no quantitative validation against real engagement for individual posts. Known biases are homogeneity (an "average persona"), more herding than humans, and model-specific asymmetries. Hybrid or diffusion-model approaches beat pure LLM-agent simulation on real cascade prediction. MiroFish is built on OASIS and inherits these limits.

### Cited Findings
- **Yang, Z. et al. (CAMEL-AI) (2024/2025). "OASIS: Open Agent Social Interaction Simulations with One Million Agents," arXiv 2411.11581 (v1 18 Nov 2024; v5 23 Mar 2025).** The simulator scales to 1M agents with dynamic follow graphs, a recommender system and X- and Reddit-like action spaces. It replicates information spreading, group polarization and herd effects. Larger agent groups give richer dynamics. Agents were more susceptible to herd effects than humans, and uncensored LLMs polarized more. — [arXiv 2411.11581](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11581v3); [project site](https://oasis.camel-ai.org)
- A commentary on OASIS notes that herd effects appear only above a certain group size, and that agents herd on dislikes but not likes, unlike humans. This may be an artifact of the base model. — [Pith review](https://pith.science/paper/2411.11581)
- "LLM-Based Social Simulations Require a Boundary" (arXiv 2506.19806) argues that LLM agents act as an "average persona" and that their behavior is homogeneous. Patterns found in a simulation may be LLM artifacts rather than social dynamics. — [arXiv 2506.19806](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19806)
- Across instantiations, LLM agents show lower variance and less behavioral diversity than humans and amplify stereotypes. — [arXiv 2507.19364](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.19364); [arXiv 2604.06663](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.06663v1)
- A contrasting position: "LLM Social Simulations Are a Promising Research Method" (arXiv 2504.02234). — [arXiv 2504.02234](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.02234)
- On real Weibo and Zhihu diffusion data, a hybrid of LLM and diffusion agents outperformed both traditional models and fully agent-based simulation. — [arXiv 2510.16366](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16366v1)
- Persona-prompted agents reach a modest MCC of 0.29 and do not beat text classifiers (see section 6). — [arXiv 2604.19787](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19787)

### Inferences
- Do not treat MiroFish or OASIS virality outputs as calibrated forecasts of LinkedIn reach. At most, use them for qualitative stress tests, such as which objections or comment threads a post might provoke. Homogeneity and herding biases will likely compress or exaggerate the spread of outcomes.
- OASIS models X and Reddit, not LinkedIn's feed and professional norms. Its recommender is not LinkedIn's ranking system.

### Gaps
- I found no published quantitative validation of OASIS cascades against real Twitter cascade-size distributions. I did not retrieve Y Social details (Rossetti et al., 2024, arXiv) or specific results on sycophancy or positivity bias in engagement simulations.
